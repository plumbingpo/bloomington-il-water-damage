Appliance Leak Water Damage Help in Bloomington

Most of the appliance leaks we see trace back to three machines. When the supply hose on a washing machine splits, water flows under full pressure until somebody turns it off. A dishwasher tends to leak slowly from a door seal or drain hose, out of sight under the cabinets. The ice maker line on a refrigerator is a thin tube, and it can drip for months behind the unit. No two of these call for the same cleanup.

For homeowners in Bloomington, we begin by sliding the machine out to see how far the water traveled. In kitchens that usually means under the cabinets and beneath the flooring. In laundry rooms it means the wall behind the machine and the rooms next door, or the ceiling below if the laundry is upstairs. Standing water is extracted, absorbed moisture is measured, and drying equipment is sized to fit the wet area.

Before We Arrive

  1. Shut the Machine DownCancel the cycle, and pull the plug only when the floor and the outlet near it are dry. If not, switch it off at the breaker instead.
  2. Close the Supply ValveWashers have hot and cold valves on the wall behind them. Look below the sink for the valve that feeds a dishwasher. Refrigerator lines have a small valve behind or below.
  3. Soak Up Standing WaterGetting reachable water up with a mop or towels stops it from moving under the cabinets and the floor. Use a vacuum only if it is a wet and dry model.
  4. Leave the Door ShutA dishwasher or washer that still holds water should stay closed. Opening the door mid-cycle can release the whole load of water onto the floor.
  5. Document What FailedPhotograph the failed fitting, valve or hose, and hold on to the part itself. Both the appliance maker and your insurer may want to look at it.
Will Insurance Pay?

Does Insurance Cover It?

When a machine overflows unexpectedly or a hose bursts, that is typically treated as sudden and accidental, which many homeowners policies cover. Payment typically goes toward the damage the water did, and the appliance or the part that failed is not included. Slow seepage from a dishwasher or ice maker line is a harder claim, because gradual leaks usually are not covered. Certain insurers will want to know the hose's age and whether it was kept up. Before cleanup, take photos, save the failed part and read your policy.

Why It Happens

How It Usually Starts

  • Standpipes that clog, or the drain hose from a washer coming loose from one.
  • An overflow caused by an overloaded drum or by using too much detergent.
  • Inlet valves that stick open, so the dishwasher or washer keeps taking in water past the fill level.
  • A clogged defrost drain or cracked drain pan that lets a refrigerator leak onto the floor.
  • A kinked or cracked ice maker line, or one whose fitting has worked loose.
If It Is Left

Where the Damage Spreads

Adjoining Rooms

For hours, a failed washer hose can keep releasing water. It passes under walls into hallways, closets and bedrooms, and soaks carpet and pad well away from the laundry room.

Floors and What Is Under Them

Water slides beneath laminate, vinyl plank and hardwood. Cupping and swelling show up in the boards, and the subfloor can stay damp long after the surface appears dry.

Downstairs Ceiling Damage

The ceiling under an upper floor laundry catches whatever water makes it past the floor above. Drywall there sags and stains while the insulation over it holds water.

Mold Behind Appliances

Hardly anyone looks at the dark, warm space behind a dishwasher or refrigerator. A slow drip there feeds mold on the wall and floor long before anyone notices.

Our crew places drying equipment and aims airflow at the hardwood and the cabinets, drying them in place whenever possible to avoid removing them.

We apply antimicrobial treatment wherever discharge from the washer or the dishwasher made contact. Final moisture readings are taken before we return the appliance, so it never sits on a damp floor.
